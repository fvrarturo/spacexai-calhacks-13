### How common is polymorphism? Every crystal in the Crystallography Open Database

*our computation on public data* · 27.1 GB processed · methods: `docs/appendix/` · result file: `data/results/bigdata/cod-polymorphs.json`

**58 %** — Of the 123 FDA-approved drugs crystallised at least twice in the open Crystallography Open Database, 72 (58%) show two or more distinct solid forms, vs 34% for organic compounds in general. A lower bound: the open database misses many known forms.

Question: How often does the same molecule crystallise in more than one solid form, and is that common among FDA-approved drugs?

Findings:
- COD archive: 521,901 CIFs parsed by our job array; 447,032 pass the quality filters, of which 169,988 are organic molecular crystals.
- Formula + name identity groups these into 166,537 compounds; 1,803 have >= 2 entries and 613 of those are polymorphic (34.0%, 95% CI 31.8-36.2%).
- Volumetric thermal expansion estimated from 663 same-compound same-space-group pairs: median 1.68e-4 per K (IQR 1.23-2.13e-4).
- Structure-key cross-check (InChIKey connectivity, single-component entries): 34.4% of 3,549 multiply-crystallised compounds are polymorphic.
- Liberal upper bound (formula only, which merges isomers): 69.3% - shows why name or structure matching matters.
- FDA Orange Book: 1,838 unique active moieties with a PubChem structure; 295 have a neat (single-component) crystal structure in COD; 123 have >= 2 entries and 72 of those show >= 2 forms (58.5%, 95% CI 49.7-66.9%).
- 361 FDA moieties also appear in COD as salts, solvates, hydrates or cocrystals - further solid forms not counted as polymorphs here.
- paracetamol (acetaminophen): 21 neat COD entries by structure (2 forms), 30 by name (3 forms); literature: 3 (forms I-III at ambient pressure (paracetamol)).
- carbamazepine: 16 neat COD entries by structure (3 forms), 0 by name (0 forms); literature: 5 (anhydrous forms I-V; Form V: Arlin et al., Chem. Commun. 47, 7074 (2011)).
- ritonavir: 0 neat COD entries by structure (0 forms), 0 by name (0 forms); literature: 2 (Forms I and II; Bauer et al., Pharm. Res. 18, 859 (2001)).
- sulfathiazole: 14 neat COD entries by structure (2 forms), 0 by name (0 forms); literature: 5 (forms I-V).
- ROY: 4 neat COD entries by structure (1 forms), 9 by name (1 forms); literature: 10 (>= 10 forms; Yu, Acc. Chem. Res. 43, 1257 (2010); more reported since).

What it does not show:
- COD is open but incomplete: the CSD holds ~1.3M+ structures vs COD's ~0.5M, and most pharmaceutical polymorph data sit in the CSD or in company files. Counts here are lower bounds.
- Most compounds were crystallised only once, so 'no second form seen' is not evidence of no polymorphism.
- Solvates, hydrates and salts have different formulas and are deliberately NOT counted as polymorphs; a mis-entered formula (e.g. solvent omitted) can still create a false polymorph.
- Name normalisation can fail both ways: different authors' systematic names for one compound do not merge (undercount), and an over-generic name could merge isomers (guarded by requiring the same formula).
- Racemate vs enantiopure crystals of one constitution can look like two forms (flagged in stats as chirality_only); temperature-induced phase transitions count as distinct forms (flagged as forms not seen at similar temperatures).
- Space-group mis-assignment and pseudo-symmetry (Z' > 1 superstructures) can split or merge forms.
- The FDA comparison is confounded by attention: drugs are screened for polymorphs on purpose. The stratified chart compares compounds with the same number of determinations, which helps but does not remove that bias.
- The Orange Book also lists amino acids, sugars and polyols (e.g. glycine, dextrose, mannitol) as active ingredients; they are included. Salts are matched through their largest organic component, so a metal salt maps to its organic ligand.
- Repeat determinations of a compound are often published because something new (a new form) was found, which inflates polymorph shares among multiply-crystallised compounds; compare with deliberate screens: Stahly, Cryst. Growth Des. 7, 1007 (2007) reports roughly half of ~245 industrially screened compounds as polymorphic (published result).
- This is about solid forms on Earth; it says nothing direct about microgravity crystallisation.

### Why gravity ruins large colloidal crystals

*physics computation (not a measurement)* · 0.0 B processed · methods: `docs/appendix/` · result file: `data/results/bigdata/colloid-sedimentation.json`

**1.1 um** — On the ground, silica spheres larger than about 1.1 um sink faster than Brownian motion can arrange them (Pe > 1). At an ISS-like 1e-6 g that limit moves to about 36 um, and at 1e-7 g to about 63 um. Physics computation, not a measurement.

Question: At what particle size does sedimentation beat Brownian self-assembly, on the ground and in orbit?

Findings:
- At 1 g the Pe = 1 crossover is at d* = 1.12 um for silica, 1.70 um for PMMA and 2.35 um for polystyrene (density-matched particles settle less).
- At 1e-6 g the silica crossover moves to 35.6 um and at 1e-7 g to 63.2 um, so every size from 100 nm to 5 um has Pe < 1 in orbit.
- 300 nm silica spheres, the size used for visible-light photonic crystals, sink 1 mm in 5.03 hours on the ground and in 573 years at 1e-6 g.
- For visible-range spheres (~200-300 nm) Pe is already below 1 on the ground, so the gravity problem for them is not single-particle settling but the dense sediment: the gravitational length (30 um for 300 nm silica) is far smaller than a mm-cm sample, giving concentration gradients and self-compression. Larger (IR, 1-5 um) spheres have Pe >> 1 on the ground.
- Published context (not our result): hard-sphere colloids that stayed glassy on Earth crystallised in microgravity on Space Shuttle STS-73 (Zhu et al., Nature 387, 883, 1997).
- Literature sample (our computation on OpenAlex abstracts): of 135 papers that state a sphere size, the median is 335 nm and 8 (5.9%) use spheres above silica's 1 g crossover of 1.1 um. Most experiments sit in the sub-micron range that visible-light photonics needs, where Pe < 1 even on the ground; the gravity-limited regime (> ~1 um, infrared photonics, large-sphere model systems) is the under-explored part. Why authors choose sizes is not in the data.

What it does not show:
- Physics computation, not a measurement or simulation of crystal growth.
- Dilute single-sphere Stokes settling; dense suspensions settle more slowly (hindered settling), and walls, convection, charge and polydispersity are ignored.
- Pe < 1 is a necessary condition for gravity not to dominate, not a sufficient condition for a large defect-free crystal.
- 1e-7 g is a placeholder; the real free-flyer level comes from the orbit workstream. ISS measured quasi-steady levels are typically a few micro-g (d* then shrinks by ~5^(1/4) = 1.5x).
- Particle densities are typical values; Stober silica density varies (~1.9-2.2 g/cm3).
- Literature sizes: abstract-only regex extraction from a seeded random sample of OpenAlex works; only papers that state a size in the abstract are counted, a few matches may be pore or film sizes rather than sphere diameters, and the sample was run from a laptop because OpenAlex's anonymous budget is per IP.

### Cell spacing measured in 54,000 ISS solidification images (DECLIC-DSI)

*our computation on public data* · 56.6 GB processed · methods: `docs/appendix/` · result file: `data/results/bigdata/dsi-morphology.json`

**53,895 ISS frames** — We measured cell spacing in 53,895 ISS images of a solidifying transparent alloy: runs settle at 223-731 um, and runs at the same pulling speed from different 2010 sequences differ 1.6x to 1.8x.

Question: Can a physically meaningful, defensible morphology metric be extracted at scale from public ISS solidification imagery?

Findings:
- We streamed and analysed 53,895 axial (HR2) frames (56.6 GB) from all 8 public DECLIC-DSI runs; the 2-D FFT spacing metric found a cellular pattern in 36,366 frames recorded during directional solidification.
- INIT-4 (V = 1 um/s): spacing 410 um over the last 40% of the run (bin-bootstrap interval 393-411 um); cells first stable after ~6.5 mm of pulling; spacing went from 353 um near 8.5 mm to 431 um at the end (+22%).
- S2-4 (V = 1 um/s): spacing 731 um over the last 40% of the run (bin-bootstrap interval 704-756 um); cells first stable after ~4.55 mm of pulling; spacing went from 505 um near 6 mm to 751 um at the end (+49%).
- S1-4 (V = 0.5 um/s): spacing 401 um over the last 40% of the run (bin-bootstrap interval 384-416 um); cells first stable after ~7.67 mm of pulling; spacing went from 474 um near 9 mm to 411 um at the end (-13%).
- S2-11 (V = 0.5 um/s): spacing 646 um over the last 40% of the run (bin-bootstrap interval 616-668 um); cells first stable after ~8.43 mm of pulling; spacing went from 491 um near 10.4 mm to 720 um at the end (+47%).
- HD-9 (V = 0.35 -> 0.75 um/s): spacing 422 um over the last 40% of the run (bin-bootstrap interval 404-428 um); cells first stable after ~29.5 mm of pulling.
- S1-1 (V = 6 um/s): no steady-state value - only 48% of late-run frames pass the gate (fast, low-contrast fronts; eye checks show cells in some frames, fringes or blur in others).
- S1-2 (V = 12 um/s): spacing 223 um over the last 40% of the run (bin-bootstrap interval 218-226 um); cells first stable after ~21.5 mm of pulling; spacing went from 246 um near 23.5 mm to 217 um at the end (-12%).
- S1-3 (V = 16 um/s): no steady-state value - only 35% of late-run frames pass the gate (fast, low-contrast fronts; eye checks show cells in some frames, fringes or blur in others).
- S1-1 (6 um/s) is bimodal: ~240 um cells for most of the run and ~550 um in the last ~8 mm (both confirmed by eye); the cause (cell-dendrite transition, end-of-pull transient or an optics change) is not identifiable from PSI metadata.
- Same pulling rate, different sequence: 0.5 um/s: S1-4 401 um, S2-11 646 um (x1.61); 1.0 um/s: INIT-4 410 um, S2-4 731 um (x1.78). Spring-2010 runs (INIT, S1) settle at ~400 um and autumn-2010 runs (S2) at 650-730 um, so pulling rate alone does not set the spacing; a different thermal gradient or growth history between sequences is the likely reason (the Task Book reports two gradients), but PSI does not record G per run.
- Across the 6 runs with a steady value, spacing ~ V^-0.26, but dropping one run moves the exponent from -0.31 to 0.08: with sequences mixed, no scaling law can be claimed.
- HD-9 (velocity jump 0.35 -> 0.75 um/s at 30 mm): the front stayed planar (no cells) for the first ~30 mm at 0.35 um/s; cells appeared just before the jump (~321 um), grew to ~592 um by 35.5 mm, then settled at ~426 um at 0.75 um/s (frames at 35 and 50 mm checked by eye).
- Two illumination looks of the same camera (dark cell-boundary view, grey cell-tip-dot view) give the same late-run spacing: INIT-4 410 vs 410 um (+0.1%), S1-4 414 vs 402 um (+3.1%), S2-4 735 vs 710 um (+3.6%), HD-9 430 vs 420 um (+2.1%) - an internal validation that the metric measures the pattern, not the optics.
- Data reduction: each 1,049,654-byte frame is summarised by 16 numbers (64 bytes as float32), a ~16,000x reduction; the whole 56.6 GB axial archive becomes 3.4 MB of morphology - the kind of step an on-board foundry pipeline could run before downlink.

What it does not show:
- No matched ground (1 g) runs are in PSI, so nothing here measures a microgravity advantage; the result shows that space imagery supports quantitative morphology at scale, and what the pattern dynamics are.
- Eight runs only; two pairs share a pulling rate but come from different sequences, and the thermal gradient G per run is not in the PSI files (the Task Book reports two G values), so V and G effects are confounded.
- The physical scale (11.51 um/px) is our disk-fit calibration assuming a 10-mm sample; a different true diameter scales all spacings proportionally (relative comparisons are unaffected).
- Frames are timed from planned command dates (the plan sheets); actual start times can lag by minutes. Mode-1 frames mix two illuminations (dark cell-boundary view and grey dot view); both show the same spacing in validation, but the order metrics differ by mode.
- The FFT spacing is a dominant-wavelength measure over a 8.8-mm field; it does not resolve local spacing gradients (centre vs edge) or individual cell neighbours.
- Frames within a run are autocorrelated; within-run intervals are descriptive, not independent-replicate confidence intervals.

### Which known MOFs have pore windows sized for lithium sieving?

*our computation on public data* · 4.6 MB processed · methods: `docs/appendix/` · result file: `data/results/bigdata/mof-pore-screen.json`

**10,804 MOFs** — 10,804 of 12,863 distinct known MOFs (84%) have pore windows wider than a water molecule (2.65 A) but narrower than a hydrated Mg2+ ion (8.56 A), the geometric range for ion sieving; only 423 sit in the narrow band between hydrated Li+ and Mg2+. Candidate chemistry is plentiful; making oriented, defect-free membranes is the open problem. Geometry only, not selectivity.

Question: How many known MOFs have pore-limiting diameters in a range useful for separating lithium from magnesium in brine?

Findings:
- CoRE MOF 2019: 10,804 of 12,863 independent MOFs (84.0%, 95% CI 83.35-84.62%) have PLD between 2.65 and 8.56 A.
- 10,381 of those have windows narrower than hydrated Li+ (2.65-7.64 A), the partial-dehydration regime; only 423 fall in the narrow 7.64-8.56 A band between the hydrated Li+ and Mg2+ diameters.
- CoRE MOF 2024 public SI set: 1,171 of 1,385 unique MOFs (84.5%) are in the same 2.65-8.56 A window.
- UiO-66: PLD 3.87 A (LCD 8.73 A) in CoRE MOF 2019 ASR, 10 entries, range 3.79-4.01 A.
- UiO-67: PLD 6.67 A (LCD 12.97 A) in CoRE MOF 2019 ASR, 12 entries, range 5.63-6.72 A.
- The CoRE MOF 2024 authors' ML model predicts water stability >= 0.5 for 874 of the 1171 in-window 2024 MOFs; that is a model output, not a measurement.

What it does not show:
- Geometric pre-screen only. PLD from rigid-framework Zeo++ geometry; real frameworks flex, swell and breathe in water, and windows shift by tenths of an angstrom.
- PLD in a band does not imply Li/Mg selectivity: dehydration energetics, charge, functional groups and transport path all matter.
- Membrane performance is dominated by growth orientation, intergrowth and defects (missing linkers, grain boundaries), none of which are in these crystal-structure databases. These are exactly what an orbital process might change, and that is a hypothesis, not a result.
- CoRE only contains frameworks with PLD >= 2.4 A, so the 'below water' band is truncated by design.
- Refcode-family and MOFid de-duplication is approximate; CoRE 2019 and CoRE 2024 may share some MOFs, so their counts are reported separately rather than summed.
- Hydrated ion sizes are model-dependent (Nightingale's values are one standard set).

### Every space-grown protein crystal in the PDB

*our computation on public data* · 92.6 GB processed · methods: `docs/appendix/` · result file: `data/results/bigdata/pdb-microgravity.json`

**67 space-grown PDB entries** — 67 PDB structures came from crystals grown in orbit. Against same-era Earth crystals of the same protein, the space one was the sharpest for 54% of 28 proteins (chance: 21%); against Earth controls from the same paper, no clear difference.

Question: Across the whole Protein Data Bank, do protein crystals grown in microgravity give better diffraction data than Earth-grown crystals of the same protein?

Findings:
- 67 of 261,139 PDB entries (208,134 X-ray) say in their own deposited text (crystal-growth fields, title or keywords) that the crystal was grown in microgravity; they cover 41 distinct proteins or nucleic acids. 2 of them merge space- and Earth-grown crystals.
- Platforms named in the text: Shuttle 12, ISS 14, Unknown 41.
- 33 proteins have both space- and Earth-grown X-ray entries. Median best-space minus best-Earth resolution: 0.13 Å (95% CI 0.00 to 0.29; Wilcoxon p = 0.073). Negative means the space crystal diffracted further.
- Against Earth entries deposited within ±3 years (28 proteins): median -0.06 Å (95% CI -0.10 to 0.09; Wilcoxon p = 0.82).
- A space-grown entry is the protein's best-resolution entry for 33% of proteins (95% CI 18-52%). Shuffling the space/Earth labels within each protein gives 16% (permutation p = 0.0085); within ±3 years: 54% observed vs 21% expected (p < 0.001).
- Permutation null for the median difference: 0.39 Å expected with no effect vs 0.13 Å observed (one-sided p < 0.001).
- Where one paper deposited both space- and Earth-grown crystals of the same protein (14 proteins), the median space-minus-Earth difference is -0.04 Å (95% CI -0.30 to 0.19).
- Single-chain, single-UniProt proteins only (24): median 0.09 Å (95% CI -0.03 to 0.29); space best for 29% vs 15% expected.
- R-free of the best space entry minus the best Earth entry (30 proteins): median 0.005 (95% CI -0.004 to 0.018), i.e. no difference in model agreement. Wilson B is reported for too few pairs (6) to compare.
- Hardware and programmes named in the deposited text: APCF (ESA) (8), PCF (4), PCDF (ESA) (4), IML / USML / LMS Spacelab (3), Spacehab (2), JAXA PCG / Kibo (2), GCB / GCF (Granada) (1), JCB (1).
- Text matching alone is unreliable: of 382 entries with any space vocabulary, 67 were really space-grown. The automatic growth-field rule flagged 53, of which 32 were right (60%; a random sample of 40 gave 23/40), and it found only 48% of the final list. Every candidate was therefore read by hand.
- Cross-check: RCSB's own full-text search for 5 space terms returns 101 entries; 61 are in our list, 30 others were in our reviewed candidate pool and judged not space-grown (Earth controls, unclear cases, other entries' mentions), and 10 were outside our pool and, read by hand, are false hits ('space group', shuttle vectors, an author named Jaxa-Chamiec, JAXA as funder). Our archive scan finds 6 of our 67 space-grown entries that this full-text search does not return.
- Dropping the 7 entries whose evidence is only a keyword or citation title: space best within ±3 years for 62% of 24 proteins vs 20% expected; all-time median difference 0.04 Å (95% CI -0.09 to 0.22).
- No space-grown entry names Mir as its platform; 4 further microgravity entries are cryo-EM fibrils rather than crystals and are not counted.

What it does not show:
- This is an archive correlation, not a controlled experiment: it does not show that microgravity caused any difference.
- Depositors are not required to say where crystals grew. Space-grown entries whose text does not say so are missed, and were counted as Earth-grown; the census is a lower bound.
- Publication and deposition selection: space crystals that failed to diffract were rarely deposited, while every ordinary Earth structure is. This biases toward space looking good; the long Earth tail biases the other way.
- Matching by UniProt accession set does not hold construct boundaries, mutations, ligands, space group, crystal form, cryo vs room temperature, beamline or detector fixed. Earth 'best' entries are often decades newer with better sources.
- Many flight programmes optimised the Earth controls in parallel, and some 'Earth' entries are those controls, so Earth crystals of flown proteins are not typical Earth crystals.
- Resolution limits are author-reported (refine.ls_d_res_high) and cutoff conventions changed over time (I/σ vs CC1/2).
- Ranking above a random entry of the same protein is not specific to space: any dedicated high-resolution effort (a methods paper, a showcase data set) would outrank ligand-soak and mutant entries. The same-paper pairs are the fairer test, and there are only 14 proteins with such pairs.
- The platform is unstated for 41 of 67 entries; 7 entries rest on a keyword or citation title only (a sensitivity analysis drops them); 2 entries merge space- and Earth-grown crystals and 5 are neutron structures, all excluded from the resolution comparison.
- Proteins with many Earth entries (lysozyme, thaumatin, insulin) are one unit each, but their best Earth entry is an extreme of a large sample; the label-permutation null is the fair comparison for that.

## 02 · Foundry

### What a free-flying foundry feels in each orbit: drag, lifetime, sunlight

*our computation on public data* · 12.5 GB processed · methods: `docs/appendix/` · result file: `data/results/bigdata/orbit-environment.json`

**16 nano-g at 500-525 km, active Sun** — From 253 million tracking records: drag on a foundry-sized free-flyer (our assumed BC 150 kg/m2) at 500-525 km is about 16 nano-g in active-Sun years and 1.5 nano-g in quiet years, 60-670 times below the ISS 1 micro-g requirement.

Question: How quiet (residual drag acceleration), how long-lived and how sunlit is each candidate low-Earth-orbit band for a free-flying foundry, measured from 68 years of tracking data?

Findings:
- In the 500-525 km band, drag on a foundry-like free-flyer (ballistic coefficient 150 kg/m2, our assumption) is about 1.6e-08 g in the high solar-activity tercile and 1.5e-09 g in the low tercile (6e-09-5e-08 g at solar high for BC 400-50). That is 62 to 667 times below the ISS 1 micro-g quasi-steady requirement (published).
- Altitude and the Sun matter about equally: the same foundry would feel 7.4e-08 g (solar high) and 1.1e-08 g (solar low) at 400-425 km, and 4.0e-09 and 3.0e-10 g at 600-625 km. Measured decay of passive objects rises 12x from the low to the high solar tercile at 500-525 km.
- Measured directly from 15,573 passive objects (debris and rocket bodies, with their own ballistic coefficients), the median object in the 500-525 km band decelerates at 1.0e-07 g in high solar activity (95% CI 9.0e-08-1.2e-07, bootstrap over 145 months) and 8.9e-09 g in low activity; those objects behave like a ballistic coefficient of about 24 kg/m2, so a heavier foundry feels proportionally less.
- The model route used for the foundry curves is checked against our data two ways: NRLMSIS 2.1 reproduces the measured decay of the Starshine 3 sphere (known mass and size, BC 60 kg/m2) with a median measured/model ratio of 0.87 (IQR 0.81-0.92, 48 ten-day windows, 2001-2003); and its solar-activity response is close to the measured one (measured/model ratio of high-to-low drag 0.60-1.22 across the 300-625 km bands).
- Drag is not the binding microgravity constraint for a free-flyer above about 400 km: gravity gradient alone is about 0.4 micro-g per metre of radial distance from the centre of mass (3 n^2 r, our analytic computation), so cartridge placement near the centre of mass and the absence of crew, exercise and pumps (vibratory regime, which on the ISS reaches over 10 milli-g at high frequency without isolation, published) matter more than the altitude.
- A dead foundry (BC 150) reenters on its own in about 2.14 years from 400 km, 5.62 from 450 km, 12.3 from 500 km (range 6.95-18.6 with solar phase), 25.9 from 550 km and 50.6 from 600 km. Only below about 443 km does a BC-150 foundry meet the 5-year post-mission disposal rule (FCC 2022, published) without propulsion; a light BC-50 design meets it up to about 506 km and a dense BC-400 design only below 392 km. Real rocket bodies took a median 3.7 years to reenter after crossing 500 km and 0.5 after 400 km (Kaplan-Meier, measured), close to the model's BC-50 values (4.49 and 0.695 yr).
- Power and heat: a dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit is sunlit 94% (500 km) to 96% (600 km) of the year with 96-106 eclipse days and about 1,430-1,613 thermal cycles per year (eclipses at most 22.7 min, around the June solstice), versus 66% sunlight and ~5,514 cycles for an ISS-like 51.6 deg orbit and 69% and ~5,119 for a 53 deg Starlink-like orbit, with eclipses up to 36.0 min every orbit (analytic geometry, simulation).
- Candidate band, as our computation and not a design decision: on these numbers a dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit at about 525-575 km balances the four constraints best if the foundry carries a deorbit capability. At 550 km, drag on a BC-150 foundry is 8e-10-9e-09 g, sunlight 95% with ~1,521 thermal cycles a year (vs ~5,514 at 51.6 deg), and 955 catalogued objects sit within 25 km (vs 9,924 in Starlink's 450-500 km region just below). Trade-offs: a dead BC-150 foundry would stay up ~26 years there (~10 for BC 50), far beyond the 5-year rule, so disposal must be propulsive. The alternative, an ISS-like ~420 km orbit, self-disposes in ~3 years and has only 546 neighbours, but drag is ~7x higher (still < 0.1 micro-g), it needs reboosts, and it sees 66% sunlight and ~5,514 eclipses a year. SSO launch costs more delta-v than 51.6 deg and has no crew-station access; and the result rests on public TLE crowding, an assumed BC and a model atmosphere checked on one sphere.

What it does not show:
- The measured objects are debris and rocket bodies, not foundries. Their g-levels are real measurements; the foundry curves rescale them through a model atmosphere (NRLMSIS 2.1) and an assumed ballistic coefficient, checked against one known-BC sphere (Starshine 3, solar-maximum years only) and the population's solar response. The absolute level is therefore uncertain by roughly the Starshine spread plus the BC assumption, which dominates (a factor 8 across BC 50-400).
- TLE B* could not be used as a ballistic coefficient: it tracks the solar cycle (~30x), so per-object ballistic coefficients of the measured population are not known; we only infer an effective population value through the model.
- Co-rotation of the atmosphere lowers the relative speed by ~4% (v^2 by ~8%) for 51.6-53 deg orbits and raises it slightly for sun-synchronous orbits; the foundry curves use the inertial speed.
- TLE mean elements smooth over each fit span; the result is an orbit-averaged, quasi-steady (<< 0.01 Hz) along-track drag. It says nothing about vibration, attitude-control, venting or crew disturbances, and does not include the gravity-gradient and rotational terms that depend on where a cartridge sits relative to the centre of mass.
- Some 'non-operational' payloads may have manoeuvred earlier in life; debris can be cross-tagged. Medians per object-month and the 30 km jump cut limit, but do not eliminate, these effects. Results for payloads are kept separate (stats are dominated by debris and rocket bodies).
- Solar terciles pool three solar cycles (22-25). Cycle-to-cycle differences and geomagnetic storms (e.g. the 2024 May storm) are inside the spread, not modelled separately.
- Lifetimes assume a constant BC, no attitude change and the 1990-2026 solar sequence repeated; real decays depend on future cycles. They are a planning estimate, not a disposal analysis.
- Power/thermal uses circular orbits and a cylindrical shadow (no penumbra, no atmosphere), which slightly underestimates eclipse length (< 1 min).
- ISS acceleration data (PIMS) could not be downloaded today; ISS numbers are published values, not our computation.

### How much ISS physical-science data NASA publishes openly (PSI bucket)

*our computation on public data* · 8.5 MB processed · methods: `docs/appendix/` · result file: `data/results/bigdata/psi-inventory.json`

**30.3 TB** — NASA's open Physical Sciences Informatics bucket holds 30.3 TB in 388,602 files from just three ISS investigations; the PK-4 plasma lab alone recorded a median 702 GB of video per science day.

Question: How much raw data does one ISS physical-science experiment produce, and how much of it is openly available?

Findings:
- The complete PSI bucket is 30.27 TB in 388,602 objects (345,940 distinct by content hash), from three ISS investigations.
- PK-4 complex-plasma data is 21.0 TB (69% of the bucket), almost all on-board video; OASIS 9.1 TB; DSI 165 GB.
- The 8 public DSI solidification activities contain 58,011 raw 1024x1024 frames (60.9 GB), 2.2-13.0 GB per day of operation; the NASA Task Book reports 39 DSI experiments in the campaign, so PSI hosts about a fifth of them.
- On its 31 science days PK-4 recorded a median 702 GB of on-board video per day (range 296-786 GB); that is ~192x the median DSI activity day, so data volume per experiment-day spans more than two orders of magnitude across facilities.

What it does not show:
- Volumes are what PSI publishes after downlink and curation, not what the instruments recorded; PSI states that some OASIS drives were partially loaded and PK-4 videos are missing sensor data for some runs.
- OASIS per-day values divide by the calendar span of each hard drive, which includes idle days; the zip archives may partly overlap extracted raw/tiff frames (we did not open them).
- DSI per-day values span the first to last frame of each activity (melting, ~1-day homogenisation and solidification), so they average over quiet and burst acquisition.
- Three investigations are not a representative sample of all ISS science; the full PSI catalogue (psi.nasa.gov, registration) lists many more.

### How often can anything come back from orbit?

*our computation on public data* · 27.6 MB processed · methods: `docs/appendix/` · result file: `data/results/bigdata/return-cadence.json`

**45 days** — Since 2021 a US-accessible capsule (Dragon, Starliner, Varda or Starfall) has landed every 45 days at the median. The fleet model assumes each of its 3 capsules can return every 12–24 hours.

Question: How often do pressurised or recoverable capsules land on Earth, how long are the gaps, and how does that compare with the fleet model's 12–24 h return cadence?

Findings:
- From 2021-01-01 to 2026-10-24 GCAT records 69 landings of LEO return capsules worldwide; 42 were US-accessible vehicles (Dragon, Starliner, Varda, Starfall).
- The median gap between US-accessible return opportunities was 45.25 days (90th percentile 107.06 days, longest 158.71 days). That is 45.3x the fleet model's synthetic 24 h per-capsule cadence (its 3 staggered capsules give a slot every 8 h system-wide).
- Of those 69 landings, 19 (3.27 per year) were vehicles whose job includes commercial downmass (Dragon cargo, Varda); the rest were crew vehicles with limited downmass, new or demo capsules, or vehicles that are not a routine channel for US commercial payloads (Soyuz, Shenzhou, Chinese satellites).
- Dragon cargo, the only routine pressurized downmass, landed 14 times (2.41 per year), with a median gap of 143.64 days.
- A product finished at a random moment would wait on average 38.7 days for the next US-accessible landing and 87.3 days for the next Dragon cargo landing; the fleet model assumes 12 h on average (half of 24 h).
- Of GCAT down cargo catalogued since 2021, about 26.2 t came back to Earth inside capsules and 58.1 t was burned on purpose with disposal vehicles.
- Varda's W-series capsules landed 5 times in the window; GCAT also lists a W-4 reentry vehicle as reentered without a recorded landing (status R).

What it does not show:
- Landing times come from GCAT phase records; some are flagged uncertain (?) and a few are day-precision only. That changes gaps by hours, not the weeks-scale conclusion.
- "US-accessible" and "commercial downmass" are our classifications from public vehicle roles, not manifests. A landing is an opportunity only if the payload was booked on that flight, often months ahead, so pooled gaps overstate real access.
- Crew Dragon, Soyuz and Starliner carry some downmass, but GCAT does not catalogue it separately for crew flights, so its mass is unknown here.
- GCAT down-cargo masses are mostly nominal (round, flagged ?) and should not be read as measured manifests.
- Varda and SpaceX Starfall are new; with a handful of flights, their cadence is not yet a stable rate.
- Failed or unrecorded recoveries (for example the Varda W-4 reentry vehicle, status R; The Exploration Company's Mission Possible, status D) are listed in stats, not counted as returns.
- The window ends at the GCAT update date, so the last gap is right-censored (the next landing has not happened yet).
- An external cross-check against Wikipedia mission lists was attempted, but the Wikimedia API rate-limited our IP. Validation here is internal: contiguous mission numbering, CRS-7 (2015 launch failure) and Soyuz MS-10 (2018 abort) are the only gaps.

### Who needs high-concentration protein formulations, and how fast is that growing?

*our computation on public data* · 2.8 GB processed · methods: `docs/appendix/` · result file: `data/results/bigdata/demand-signals.json`

**16 antibodies** — Since 2020 the FDA has licensed subcutaneous versions of 16 antibodies first licensed as IV infusions (Keytruda Qlex in Sep 2025), and registered SC antibody trials grew from 31 starting in 2005 to about 248 a year in 2021-2025.

Question: Is there real, growing demand for the formulations orbit-grown protein crystals could serve (subcutaneous high-dose antibodies), and for the commodities our other candidates touch (lithium)?

Findings:
- Across the full ClinicalTrials.gov registry (608,415 studies), 25,470 interventional studies test an INN '-mab' antibody.
- Studies that give an antibody subcutaneously grew from 31 starting in 2005 to 248 a year on average in 2021-2025 (about 8.0x).
- The SC share of route-stated antibody studies was 15% in 2005-2009, peaked at 34% in 2014, and was 21% in 2020-2025 (95% CI 20-22%).
- Part of that dip is the IV checkpoint-inhibitor boom: PD-1/PD-L1/CTLA-4/LAG-3 antibodies went from 2% of antibody-level IV mentions in 2005 to 50% in 2020; excluding them the SC share is 30% in 2020 (vs 40% in 2014), so it still fell, but less. These IV checkpoint antibodies are the pool now being converted to SC (nivolumab, atezolizumab, pembrolizumab).
- With full words only (no abbreviations) the shares are higher (26% in 2005-2009, 29% in 2020-2025) because IV is more often abbreviated; the rise-then-plateau shape is the same.
- The FDA Purple Book lists 21 antibodies licensed IV first that later gained an SC product under a 351(a) (non-biosimilar) licence; 16 of those SC licences came in 2020 or later: daratumumab (Darzalex Faspro, 2020-05), pertuzumab (Phesgo, 2020-06), ofatumumab (Kesimpta, 2020-08), ravulizumab (Ultomiris, 2022-06), vedolizumab (Entyvio, 2023-09), infliximab (Zymfentra, 2023-10), spesolimab (Spevigo, 2024-03), atezolizumab (Tecentriq Hybreza, 2024-09), ocrelizumab (Ocrevus Zunovo, 2024-09), nivolumab (Opdivo Qvantig, 2024-12), lecanemab (Leqembi IQLIK, 2025-08), pembrolizumab (Keytruda Qlex, 2025-09), mosunetuzumab (Lunsumio Velo, 2025-12), amivantamab (Rybrevant Faspro, 2026-02), anifrolumab (Saphnelo, 2026-04), isatuximab (Sarclisa Escena, 2026-07).
- New originator antibody licences that include an SC product at first approval: 3/20 in 1994-2004, 6/25 in 2005-2014, 25/52 in 2015-2019 and 31/86 in 2020-2026 (FDA Purple Book).
- 9 licensed antibody products are co-formulated with a hyaluronidase: today's main route to high-dose SC antibodies is enzyme-assisted injection, not crystalline suspensions.
- ClinicalTrials.gov holds 143 studies (start 2005-2026) of antibodies co-formulated with a hyaluronidase or named by an SC brand/code, a direct count of IV-to-SC reformulation programmes.
- World lithium mine production rose from 32,200 t (2015) to an estimated 290,000 t in 2025 (9.0x, USGS, lithium content, excluding the US).
- Hand check of the route classifier: 18/20 SC calls and 19/20 IV calls were correct on a deterministic sample; 38.2% of sampled antibody studies state no route at all.

What it does not show:
- Registered trials are a proxy for formulation demand. They do not measure doses, patients, sales or willingness to pay, and they say nothing yet about demand for orbit-grown crystals specifically.
- Route is inferred from free text with regular expressions. Precision is reported from a hand check, recall is lower: 30-40% of antibody studies state no route in the fields we read, and those are excluded from the share.
- The '-mab' INN stem misses antibodies known only by code names (e.g. MK-3475 alone) and antibodies named under the 2021+ INN scheme (-tug, -bart, -mig, -ment); 'biologic' (interventionType BIOLOGICAL) also includes vaccines and cell therapies.
- Start year is not approval year; 2026 is a partial year and includes planned (estimated) start dates. Registration practice changed over time (ICMJE 2005, FDAAA 2007, the 2017 Final Rule), so early-year counts are not strictly comparable.
- One study counts once even if it has many arms; studies are not independent of each other (one sponsor runs many related trials of one molecule).
- Purple Book counts are licences by route, not sales; route is read from the product listing, and our IV-to-SC list is restricted to originator (351(a)) products. Drugs@FDA covers only CDER BLAs and its route field is often just 'INJECTION', so it is used only as a date cross-check.
- SC demand is being met today mainly by hyaluronidase co-formulation and high-concentration liquids. Crystalline suspensions (Merck's published pembrolizumab work) are one alternative; this analysis does not show they will win.
- Lithium: production is a supply series, US consumption is withheld after 2021, the 2023 value is an MCS 2024 estimate, and 2024-2025 are USGS estimates. It does not show demand for MOF membranes.
- No solid-state / barocaloric cooling demand series was added: we found no quick, real public market series and did not substitute one.

### Launch scale and the SpaceX effect

*our computation on public data* · 44.5 MB processed · methods: `docs/appendix/` · result file: `data/results/bigdata/launch-scale.json`

**51.7 %** — In 2025, SpaceX flew 51.7% of the world's 329 orbital launch attempts and carried 85.0% of the 4,570 payloads that reached orbit.

Question: How much launch capacity exists today, how fast is it growing, and how much of it is SpaceX?

Findings:
- GCAT lists 7,414 orbital launch attempts from 1957 to 2026-10-24; 2025 had 329, 3.78x the 87 of 2015.
- SpaceX flew 170 of the 329 orbital launch attempts in 2025 (51.7%).
- 4,570 payloads reached orbit in 2025; 85.0% rode SpaceX rockets and 3,193 were Starlink.
- Catalogued payload mass placed in orbit in 2025 sums to 3,096.4 t (84.8% on SpaceX launches), 10.28x the 301.1 t of 2015; 100.0% of 2025 payloads have a mass in GCAT.
- Through Oct 24: 252 orbital attempts in 2026 (122 by SpaceX) vs 250 (139 by SpaceX) in the same window of 2025: growth has paused this year in GCAT's count.
- 23 Transporter/Bandwagon rideshare missions carried 1,742 catalogued payloads (median 74 per mission, range 4–145).
- Starship has made 14 integrated flights (10 coded successful by GCAT). GCAT puts 11 of them (flight 4 onward, including two failures) in its orbital category (target perigee above zero within uncertainties); the successful ones have GCAT perigees of -40 to 8 km, i.e. at the edge of orbit.

What it does not show:
- 2026 is partial (data to 2026-10-24); year-to-date comparisons use the same calendar window.
- Launch counts follow GCAT definitions (McDowell); other trackers differ by a few launches a year on borderline cases (Starship, failures before orbit).
- Mass sums use payload launch masses, many of which are GCAT estimates (flagged ?), and classified payloads with nominal masses. They exclude upper stages, so they understate total mass to orbit. Coverage per year is reported.
- SpaceX share counts the launch provider. Payload owners differ: Starlink is SpaceX-owned, while rideshare payloads belong to hundreds of organisations.
- Recent rideshare missions may not be fully catalogued yet, so their payload counts are lower bounds.
- This is capacity *up*. A foundry also needs capacity *down*, which is far scarcer: see return-cadence.json.

### The literature no human can read: 50 years of microgravity process papers

**11 papers** — Of 4,532 papers pairing microgravity with a foundry process, just 11 concern crystallizing small molecules or drug ingredients in microgravity, and 10 of those are from 2024 or later. The pharma-crystal frontier is brand new.

Question: How much published work pairs microgravity or spaceflight with each foundry process, who writes it, and which partners does the record actually support?

Findings:
- 4532 distinct OpenAlex works pair a space-environment term with one of the six foundry processes in their title or abstract (keyword hits; 110401 works match the space terms alone).
- Solidification/alloy dominates the keyword set (2709 works, spot-check precision 15/20), followed by in-space manufacturing (978, 13/20) and protein crystallization (576, 14/20).
- Only 11 works concern small-molecule crystals (APIs, pharmaceutical seed crystals, one pigment polymorph) or pharmaceutical-ingredient manufacturing in microgravity, after hand-reading all 132 pharma keyword hits; 10 of them appeared in 2024 or later. This is a new and very thin literature.
- MOF growth in (simulated) microgravity has 3 records (2 distinct studies). 9 of the 13 MOF+space hits are about using MOFs aboard spacecraft. Zeolites, the closest porous-crystal precedent, have 141 keyword hits with spot-check precision 8/20, much of it from 1987-2003 Shuttle/EURECA work.
- Top institutions by distinct papers: Marshall Space Flight Center (434), Glenn Research Center (172), Deutsches Zentrum für Luft- und Raumfahrt e. V. (DLR) (156).
- Companies are on 354 of 4532 works (7.8%). The most frequent are Maruwa Foods and Biosciences (Japan) (21), Confocal Science (Japan) (17), Boeing (United States) (16), Advanced Engineering Services (Japan) (13), Northrop Grumman (United States) (12).
- Company share by era (works with at least one company affiliation): 1980-1999 98/1161; 2000-2014 115/1436; 2015-2025 107/1494.
- In-space manufacturing papers peaked in 2025 among complete years: 529 in 2016-2025 vs 80 in 2006-2015.
- Candidate partners with zero affiliated works in this set: Merck KGaA (Darmstadt), Space Tango, ACME Advanced Materials / Astral Materials, Sierra Space / Sierra Nevada Corp. A zero is not evidence of no activity.
- The small-molecule frontier is concentrated: Redwire is on 5 and Butler University on 5 of the 11 papers, Varda on 1, and 1 carries no affiliation data at all.
- Normalised to all OpenAlex works, the six-topic union went from 33.0 per million works in the 1990s (the Shuttle/Spacelab era) to 10.5 in the 2010s and 12.8 in 2020-2025. The five materials topics alone went 31.5 to 8.3 to 7.5, so the recent uptick is mostly in-space manufacturing, while materials-in-microgravity research is a steady niche.
- SpaceX is a verified author affiliation on 0 union works and 1 in the narrow pharma set (drug content after a commercial EVA). OpenAlex attributes two other works to SpaceX by mistake, so its institution field alone cannot be trusted here.

What it does not show:
- Keyword search over titles and abstracts (OpenAlex stemmed title_and_abstract.search). Precision was measured by reading a seeded random sample of 20 titles per topic (all 13 for MOF); recall was not measured, so works that never use our phrases are missed.
- Precision varies widely: Protein crystallization 14/20 (95% Wilson 0.481-0.855); Pharmaceutical crystallization / polymorph 2/20 (95% Wilson 0.028-0.301); Solidification / alloy 15/20 (95% Wilson 0.531-0.888); Colloid / photonic crystal 12/20 (95% Wilson 0.387-0.781); Metal-organic framework 3/13 (95% Wilson 0.082-0.503); In-space manufacturing 13/20 (95% Wilson 0.433-0.819); Pharmaceutical crystallization, narrow phrase set 5/20 (95% Wilson 0.112-0.469); Zeolite (porous-crystal precedent for MOFs) 8/20 (95% Wilson 0.219-0.613).
- The on-topic judgements and the small-molecule/MOF classification are ours (an AI agent reading titles, plus abstracts from free single-work lookups where unclear). They are not peer-reviewed.
- OpenAlex mis-resolves some affiliation strings (hand-verified examples in stats.affiliation_parsing_errors_found: a Redwire author resolved to SpaceX, NASDA to an agricultural association). Institution counts therefore carry noise of a few works per organisation.
- OpenAlex affiliation coverage is incomplete: 70% of works have at least one resolved institution, and 5510 of 17388 authorships have none (1358 of those still carry a raw affiliation string, which we search for partners). Institution parsing errors and mergers (e.g. Techshot and Made In Space into Redwire) split counts.
- 2026 is a partial year, and recent works are still being indexed.
- The per-million normalisation divides by every OpenAlex record, and OpenAlex's coverage of non-article records grows over time, so the normalised decline partly reflects indexing, not only attention.
- Records include conference abstracts, NASA technical reports, journal covers, corrections and duplicates (e.g. three 'Manufacturing in the Void' records), so counts are works, not independent studies.
- Company affiliation counts undercount industry work: most pharma and materials process data is unpublished, patented, or released as press material.
- Without an API key OpenAlex refuses cursor paging past page 1, so pages 2+ used basic page=N paging. For solidification this returned 2709 of 2712 works (the API's own group_by counts per year are stored in stats for comparison).
- A correlation between publication volume and any market is not established here. Paper counts measure research attention, not demand or technical maturity.

### How crowded each low-Earth-orbit shell is, 1990 to today

**27,298 catalogued objects below 2,000 km** — On 2026-10-23 the public catalogue tracks 27,298 objects in low Earth orbit, up from 4,846 in 1990 and 15,094 in 2020; 92% of the objects between 450 and 500 km are Starlink satellites.

Question: Which altitude shells would a foundry fleet share, with whom, and how fast are they filling?

Findings:

- The most crowded 25 km shell today is 450-475 km with 6,144 objects, then 475-500 km (3,780); both are mostly Starlink. The next most crowded shells are the old debris belts at 750-850 km.
- Candidate foundry bands today: 400-450 km: 546, 450-500 km: 9,924, 500-550 km: 985, 550-600 km: 983, 600-650 km: 1,157, 650-700 km: 860 objects.

What it does not show:
- Public TLE catalogue only (~10 cm and larger in LEO); classified objects and small debris are missing, and tracking capacity grew over time (1990s counts are lower partly for that reason).
- Snapshot uses objects with an element set in the prior 30 days; objects with sparse tracking can drop out of a given year.
- Mean-altitude assignment understates how many shells eccentric objects cross, and 'altitude' here is a - 6378.137 km, typically 5-10 km below altitudes quoted by operators (e.g. Starlink shells announced by SpaceX at ~480 km appear at 450-475 km).
- Object counts are not collision risk; they ignore relative velocity, size and the fact that active satellites manoeuvre.

## Pitch guidance: what each result lets us say

Use this for the video, the Devpost text and the judge walkthrough. Every "say" line is backed by a result file. Every "avoid" line is a claim the data does not support.

### Materials
- **Protein crystals (whole PDB).**
  - *Say:* we parsed all 261,139 PDB entries (92.6 GB) and read 382 candidates by hand. 67 structures came from crystals grown in orbit, on Shuttle, ISS/Kibo and ESA APCF/PCDF hardware. Against same-era Earth crystals of the same protein, the space crystal was the sharpest for 54% of 28 proteins, where label shuffling predicts 21% (p < 0.001). Against Earth controls from the same paper (14 proteins), there is no clear difference.
  - *Avoid:* "microgravity makes better crystals" (this is an archive correlation), and "space crystals beat the best Earth crystals" (against the all-time best, a later Earth crystal usually wins, +0.13 Å). The census is a lower bound. Merck does not appear in the PDB data; cite its 2019 paper separately.
- **Drug crystals (COD).**
  - *Say:* among FDA drugs crystallised at least twice in the open Crystallography Open Database, 58% show two or more solid forms, versus 34% of organic compounds in general. This holds at every number of entries per compound, and it is a lower bound.
  - *Avoid:* "59% of all drugs are polymorphic", and any microgravity claim from this file. Ritonavir is absent from COD.
- **Solidification on the ISS (NASA PSI / DECLIC-DSI).**
  - *Say:* we measured cell spacing in 53,895 ISS images. Runs settle at 223–731 µm, and runs at the same pulling speed from different 2010 sequences differ 1.6–1.8×. Each 1 MB frame reduces to 16 numbers.
  - *Avoid:* a microgravity advantage (there are no matched Earth runs), a spacing–velocity law (leave-one-out flips the exponent), and calling it a cooling material (it is an SCN–camphor analogue).
- **MOF membranes (CoRE MOF).**
  - *Say:* 84% of the 12,863 distinct MOFs in CoRE MOF 2019 have pore windows between a water molecule and a hydrated Mg²⁺ ion. Only 3.3% sit in the narrow band between hydrated Li⁺ and Mg²⁺. Geometry is not the bottleneck; orientation and defects are, and orbit might change those.
  - *Avoid:* selectivity predictions, and adding the 2019 and 2024 counts together.
- **Photonic colloids (physics).**
  - *Say:* on the ground, gravity beats Brownian motion for silica spheres above about 1.1 µm. At ISS levels the crossover moves to about 36 µm.
  - *Avoid:* calling it a measurement. For ~300 nm visible-light spheres the ground problem is the compressed sediment, not single-particle settling. The 1e-7 g free-flyer level is a placeholder.

### Foundry
- **Orbit environment (TLE history).**
  - *Say:* from 253 million tracking records, drag on a foundry-sized free-flyer at 500–525 km is about 16 nano-g in active-Sun years and 1.5 nano-g in quiet years. That is 60–670× below the ISS 1 µg requirement. A passive foundry re-enters within 5 years only below about 443 km.
  - *Avoid:* "measured microgravity of our foundry". It is orbit-averaged along-track drag only, rescaled with an assumed ballistic coefficient. Gravity gradient (about 0.4 µg per metre) outweighs drag above about 450 km.
- **ISS data volume (PSI inventory).**
  - *Say:* NASA's open PSI bucket holds 30.3 TB from three ISS investigations. PK-4 alone recorded a median 702 GB of video per science day, which is why we measure on board.
  - *Avoid:* "we processed 30 TB". We catalogued it. We processed the 56.6 GB of DSI frames.
- **Return cadence (GCAT).**
  - *Say:* since 2021, a capsule a US commercial payload could ride has landed every 45 days at the median. Return, not launch, is the bottleneck.
  - *Avoid:* stating a purpose for SpaceX Starfall, and presenting the fleet model's on-time rates as achievable with today's vehicles.

### Network
- **Launch scale (GCAT).**
  - *Say:* in 2025 SpaceX flew 170 of 329 orbital launch attempts (52%) and carried 85% of the payloads that reached orbit. 23 Transporter/Bandwagon rideshares carried 1,742 payloads (median 74).
  - *Avoid:* calling Starship operational, and claiming 2026 growth (attempts are flat against 2025 to date).
- **Demand (ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA, USGS).**
  - *Say:* since 2020 the FDA has licensed subcutaneous versions of 16 antibodies first licensed as IV infusions, including Keytruda. Subcutaneous antibody trials grew about 8× from 2005. World lithium production grew 9× from 2015 to 2025.
  - *Avoid:* "the subcutaneous share is rising" (it is flat at ~21%), and claiming demand for orbit-made crystals or a MOF-membrane market.
- **Literature and partners (OpenAlex).**
  - *Say:* 4,532 works pair microgravity with a foundry process. Only 11 are about drug crystals in microgravity, and 10 of those since 2024. Only 2 grow MOFs in microgravity. The frontier is open.
  - *Avoid:* "papers on X" without the keyword-precision caveat; citing OpenAlex as evidence that SpaceX publishes here; treating zero papers as zero activity.
- **Crowding (TLE history).**
  - *Say:* 27,298 catalogued objects sit below 2,000 km today, up from 4,846 in 1990, and 92% of objects at 450–500 km are Starlink.
  - *Avoid:* treating counts as collision risk.
